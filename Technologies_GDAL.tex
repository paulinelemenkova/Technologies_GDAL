% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{Libraries with prewritten codes optimize the workflow in cartography and reduce labour intensive data processing by iteratively applying scripts to implementing mapping tasks. Most existing GIS approaches are based on traditional software with a graphical user's interface which significantly limits their performance. Although plugins are proposed to improve the functionality of many GIS, they are usually ad hoc in finding specific mapping solutions, e.g., cartographic projections and data conversion. We address this limitation by applying the principled approach of GDAL, PROJ and GRASS GIS for geospatial data processing and morphometric analysis. This research presents topographic analysis of the dataset using scripting methods which include several tools: 1) GDAL, a translator library for raster and vector geospatial data formats used for converting ETOPO1 GeoTIFF in XY Cartesian coordinates into WGS84 by the ‘gdalwarp’ utility; 2) PROJ projection transformation library used for converting ETOPO1 WGS84 grid to cartographic projections (Cassini-Soldner equirectangular; Equal Area Cylindrical; 2-point Equidistant Azimuthal; Oblique Mercator); 3) GRASS GIS by sequential use of the following modules: r.info, d.mon, d.rast, r.colors, d.rast.leg, d.legend, d.northarrow, d.grid, d.text, g.region, r.contour. The depth frequency was analysed by the module 'd.histogram'. The proposed approach provided a systematic way for morphometric measuring of topographic data and combining the advantages of the GDAL, PROJ and GRASS GIS tools that include the informativeness, effectiveness and representativeness in spatial data processing. The morphometric analysis included the computed slope, aspect, profile and tangential curvature of the study area. The data analysis revealed the distribution pattern in topographic data: 24\% of data with elevations below 400 m; 13\% of data with depths -5,000 to -6,000 m; 4\% of depths have values -3,000 to -4,000 m, the least frequent data (-6,000 to 7,000 m) <1\%, 2\% of depths have values -2,000 to 3,000 m in the basin, while other values are distributed proportionally. Further, by incorporating the generic coordinate transformation software library PROJ, we transformed raster grid into various cartographic projections to demonstrate distortions in shape and area. Scripting techniques of GRASS GIS are demonstrated for applications in topographic modelling and raster data processing. The GRASS GIS shows the effectiveness for mapping and visualization, compatibility with libraries (GDAL, PROJ), technical flexibility in combining GUI and command-line data processing. The research contributes to the technical cartographic development.}

\section{Introduction}

The spatial analysis in GIS focuses on complex problems regarding the topographic analysis and surface modelling. Conducting data searches and capture, then finding optical GIS software with full functionality of geoinformatics, performing data visualization, analysis and modelling, processing and mapping are the vital parts of cartographic workflow, which is constantly developing in multiple application of GIS  \cite{Boulton,Buterez,Lemenkova2021a,Duncan,Lemenkova2020a,Hernandez,Kiani,Teixeira,Savulescu}. Due to the variety of the existing GIS (e.g. ArcGIS, QGIS, MapINFO, ENVI, Idrisi, SAGA GIS, Erdas Imagine or scripting approaches of GMT), the choice of the tools may face many questions regarding the advantages of the application of GIS. However, a certain advantages of GIS may be the most important factors, such as open source availability and functionality of GIS including a variety of tools and embedded computational algorithms \cite{Armstrong}. The commercial GIS (e.g. Erdas Imagine, ArcGIS) may cause frequently encountered problems of access, for example in public education or for personal usage. 

On the contrary, the free availability of GIS may cause its popularity and create an increased demand of the reported examples of its applications used in a geospatial studies \cite{Christl,Turton} including programming coding and scripting, machine learning and automatization, artificial intelligence and big data processing as pointed out in a variety of contemporary studies on geoinformatics \cite{Barnes,Issaka}. The benefits of the open source GIS and scripting are widely discussed including easily data integration in a number of scenarios and geospatial research, handling 2D and 3D raster datasets, spatial information exchange and processing \cite{Ajvazi,Brovelli,Guan,Kralidis}. 

Open source data (or at least, low cost data), scripting approaches and methods used in geographic research and Earth science education \cite{Rey,Basith,Lemenkov,Habib} tend to become progressively popular and perspective in view of the increased forms of the distance-based GIS education and flexible learning context \cite{Balram}. Common sources of open geodata are topographic and bathymetric grids (GEBCO, SRTM, ETOPO1, ETOPO5), geophysical datasets (e.g. EGM96, EGM2008), geological surveys, local data from the online repositories, embedded datasets, OpenStreetMap of Google Earth imagery, remote sensing data (e.g., Landsat TM or Sentinel-2A), professional and governmental repositories, personal findings and data received from the fieldwork.

Cartographers have access to an ever-growing pool of such datasets that focuses on discipline-specific topics (e.g. geomorphology, soils, hydrology, climate changes, land cover studies, geology, etc.) as well as various GIS software and references from other related geoscience disciplines. The open source geospatial data available online (e.g.,GEBCO, SRTM, ETOPO1, various DEM) to assist in GIS mapping are wide and varied. Many of these datasets are now available in digital formats (both raster of vector ones) and commonly accessed through the internet via geospatial data repositories. The open source geospatial data describe a collaboration of datasets in Earth sciences (topography, geology, soils, climate and environmental surveys). Increasingly, geospatial data sources are developing online repositories for their data storage such as the NOAA, GloVis (remote sensing data as satellite imagery), GEBCO, etc. Availability of geospatial data access to a cartographer is determined by their open source access (e.g. OpenStreetMaps, Google Earth, Landsat TM) or embedded datasets (country boundaries as basic GIS layers). There is a wide variety of Earth scientists who do regularly access such resources and use them in GIS-based analysis.

In view of this, the question of how to better process these data, and which GIS to select, becomes the actual and emerging question in contemporary cartography. This study presents the use of GRASS GIS, an open source powerful GIS instrument for topographic analysis, visualization, statistical data analysis and mapping with a case study of the Kuril-Kamchatka region. Besides being a free software, GRASS GIS includes a variety of functional tools for processing both raster and vector data, using both GUI and scripting approach for mapping. Leaving apart the GUI approach as more trivial and easy approach of geospatial data processing, this paper concerns the scripting methods in GRASS GIS with a special case of topographic modelling and manipulation with data using an integration of GRASS GIS with such libraries as GDAL and PROJ the key tools for processing geospatial data formats and projections. 

\section{Case Study}

The study area of this research encompasses Kuril-Kamchatka Trench and Kuril island arc which extends in north-west Pacific Ocean, from the Hokkaido Island to the Kamchatka Peninsula, Fig. \ref{Fig:1}. This region is characterised by the complex geological settings and high seismicity. Almost all of islands from the Greater Kuril Chain are located along the submarine ridge of the Kuril island arc with depths mostly 100-300 m above them. The geomorphological structures include the submerged Vityaz ridge, which extends from the oceanward side of the Sea of Okhotsk. The Kuril-Kamchatka Trench generally has a flat seabed consisting of a chain of relatively wide and narrow sections of the trench with depths of over -7,000 m. However, it differs in the southern and northern parts. The maximum depth of the trench (-9,717 m) is detected in its southwestern segment.

The basement surface of the Mesozoic structures near the northern coast of the Sea of Okhotsk forms a sub-latitudinal depression with depths reaching up to 3 km. Local minor trenches (depths up to -2.000 m) extend from this depression in a sub-meridional direction, covering structural uplift in the center of the sea. The southern part of the Sea of Okhotsk has a depression with depths exceeding -5,000 m. The steep basement structures of the nearby shelf and the Kuril island arc descend towards the bottom of this depression. The geomorphology of the surface of the seafloor basement in the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench correlates with its modern relief. However, its depths at the bottom of the trench seafloor are about -1,000 to -2,000 m deeper. According to the age of the constituent rocks, the Kuril-Kamchatka island arc was formed during the Oligocene period. Some of the most ancient seamounts were formed on the Mesozoic crust reached Kuril-Kamchatka Trench (located on the slopes of the trench). 

Following further subduction of the tectonic plates under island arcs, these seamounts should be cut off from the basement surface and ‘imprinted’ in the structures of the island arcs or coastal structures of active continental margins. Such volcanic mountains are found in the structures of the submarine slopes of Kamchatka Peninsula. Geophysical settings in the study area have a zone of linear positive and negative magnetic anomalies with isometric magnetic field in the Kuril basin, which extends along the Kuril island arc and continue further within the Kamchatka Peninsula. The upper edges of the anomaly-forming bodies, associated with volcanic rocks of the Pliocene-Quaternary age, lie closer to the surface. This briefly demonstrates connectivity and correlation of the submarine and terrestrial geomorphology in the geological complexity of the region of the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench and Kuril island arc.

\section{Materials and Methods}

Methodologically, the present research was implemented in the GRASS GIS software \cite{NetelerMitasova2008}. The GRASS GIS is the open source raster and vector GIS, aș well as image and graphics production system \cite{Neteleretal2008,Neteler2000}. The GRASS GIS combines the traditional GIS menu, that is, GUI with CLI run from the console, which is convenient for the user, because it allows cartographic and analytic experimentation with layout. All maps made in GRASS GIS were stored using a UTM map coordinate system within a geographic study area of the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench region. The full scripts used in this study for topographic mapping by the GRASS GIS are available in the GitHub repository of the authors with an open access: \href{https://github.com/paulinelemenkova/Mapping_KKT_GRASS_GIS_Scripts}{https://github.com/paulinelemenkova/Mapping\_KKT\_GRASS\_GIS\_Scripts}.

The GRASS GIS uses modular system organised by name according to their functionality class (raster, vector) with the first letter indicating a function class, separated by dots and describing the specific task performed by this module.This feature is specific for GRASS GIS, similar to GMT, a scripting cartographic toolset \cite{Wesseletal2019}, which differs them from the traditional GIS. Some GIS reply on the practice of using GUI, while GRASS GIS enables to use both GUI with a standard menu and a scripting approach, which is one of the advantages of the GRASS GIS demonstrating its methodological flexibility between the fully console-based GMT and the conventional GIS. This helps to perform cartographic work in both way depending on the cartographer's preferences, particularly if they are, for example, students or the beginner learners of GIS. This paper therefore highlights the limitations in using GMT -- a fully scripting toolset and the technical advantages of GRASS GIS which profits from the combination of both approaches.

\subsection{Data capture and organization}

The data used in this project includes the open datasets available in the online sources via internet. A georeferenced TIFF file of the ETOPO1 grid by NOAA was downloaded from the publicly available source: (\href{https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/mgg/global/}{NOAA}) and used in this research as a base map following the exiting examples of the applications of NOAA products in geosciences \cite{Lemenkova2020f} (ETOPO1 Bedrock: Grid of Earth's surface depicting the bedrock underneath the ice sheets), Fig. \ref{Fig:1}. The information on the available data for raster maps was performed by the $‘g.list -f rast’$ GRASS GIS module. The information on the data grids was checked up by command ‘r.info’ module, which returns the min/max coordinates of the study area, its resolution, number of classes, cartographic projection, scale, type of map, geometry (number of centroids, points, lines, boundaries and islands), 3D or 2D.

The ‘g.region -p’ command was used to retrieve information on the data extension and selected region (WESN, number of rows, columns and cells, aș well aș additional cartographic data: ellipsoid, datum, zone).

\subsection{Data processing by GDAL}

The initial operations with raster file were performed by the GDAL, a translator library for raster and vector geospatial data formats (GDAL documentation: https://gdal.org/ The input to the reference list \cite{GDAL/OGR} which was run from the console of the GMT. In general, GDAL was used to re-project raster files to the supported projection, to perform image mosaicing and transformations (Fig. \ref{Fig:2}). In this study, three GDAL utilities were used in the following code:\\ 
$‘gdalwarp’, ‘gdal\_translate’ and ‘gdalinfo’$ command (Fig. \ref{Fig:2}). The ‘gdalwarp’ utility was used to re-projection and warping of the initial original file (ETOPO1\_Bed\_g\_geotiff.tif) from XY Cartesian coordinates to the WGS84 \cite{Defense}. 

The transformation uses the EPSG number which in this case is 4326 (corresponds to the WGS84). The warping was done by the command: $gdalwarp -t\_srs EPSG:4326 \\ETOPO1\_Bed\_g\_geotiff.tif ETOPO1\_WGS84.tif$. The next step contains using the utility ‘gdal\_translate’  (Fig. \ref{Fig:3}) with the argument $‘-projwin\_srs <srs\_def>’$, which specifies the projwin SRS GDAL in which it interprets the coordinates (here, given coordinates in the EPSG system). 

Using the gdal\_translate (Fig. \ref{Fig:3}), the file was subset (i.e. cut off the target square area from the whole big file with clockwise going coordinates (in degrees), W=140.0000 N=60.0000 E=170.0000 S=40.0000), but not reprojected: the re-projection was done in the previous step by the ‘grdalwarp’ utility. Now the subsetting of the target area was done by the following command: $gdal\_translate -projwin 140.0000 60.0000 \\170.0000 40.0000 ETOPO1\_WGS84.tif ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84.tif.$

\subsection{Transforming raster grid into cartographic projections by PROJ}

 Originally made as a technical tool for cartographic projections, PROJ gradually transformed into being a functional generic coordinate transformation engine. The supported projections were checked up by command ‘cs2cs -lp’ from the GRASS GIS console \cite{Evenden}, PROJ, 2020). \subsection{Calculating morphometric parameters}

At the next step, a slope, aspect, profile and tangential curvature maps were computed. All these geomorphic indices have been derived from the topographic grid, using the embedded formulae in the GRASS GIS. The slope shows the steepness or the degree of the topographic surface covering study area of Kamchatka region. The aspect (exposure) is the compass direction that a slope faces (East, West, South, North). Geographically, the aspect of a slope has significant impact on the local microclimate which affects physical and often botanical features of the mountain slope, i.e., a slope effect on the terrestrial areas. 

This is owing to a strong correlation of the mountain slope with temperature, which in turn is affected by the angle of the sun. Hence, both parameters are derivative from the slope of the elevation with variations in the mathematical approach and angle direction of the gradient angle (direct and perpendicular). Theoretical foundations of the slope and angle modelling in geospatial research can further be found in existing works reporting, for example on cell size \cite{Hodgson1995}, gradient angles \cite{Hodgson1998}, numerical terrain analysis \cite{Zevenbegen}, quantifying land surface and slope curvatures \cite{Dikau,Dunn,Guth,Heerdegan}. 
 
The curvature shows the rate of variations of the slope, which makes it the second function of an elevation variable related to the surface heights \cite{Schmidt}. This process is the same technically whether the purpose is profile curvature or tangential curvature, but different in mathematical algorithms approaches. So, the difference between the profile and tangential curvature consists in the method of calculation which for the last one is computed as perpendicular to the slope gradient while profile curvature measures curvature of the surface in the direction of gradient \cite{Gallant}. 

Thus, the correlation between the profile and tangential curvature reflects the algorithm approaches concerned the mathematical approximation of the geomorphological profile of the slopes and inflation of terrain heights. The differences in calculus are well illustrated in equation \ref{pc} for the profile curvature $K_P$ \cite{Golden} and equation \ref{tc} for tangential curvature $K_T$ \cite{Golden}. Formulae are given for the profile curvature in Equation \ref{pc} and for the tangential curvature in Equation \ref{tc}, respectively. 

\begin{equation}
  \label{pc}
 K_P=\frac{\left(\frac{\delta^2 z}{\delta x^2}\right) \left(\frac{\delta z}{\delta x}\right)^2 + 2\left(\frac{\delta^2 z}{\delta x \delta y}\frac{\delta z}{\delta x}\frac{\delta z}{\delta y} \right) + \frac{\delta^2 z}{\delta y^2} \left(\frac{\delta z}{\delta x}\right)^2}{pq^\frac{3}{2}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \label{tc}
 K_T=\frac{\left(\frac{\delta^2 z}{\delta x^2}\right) \left(\frac{\delta z}{\delta y}\right)^2 - 2\left(\frac{\delta^2 z}{\delta x \delta y}\frac{\delta z}{\delta x}\frac{\delta z}{\delta y} \right) + \frac{\delta^2 z}{\delta y^2} \left(\frac{\delta z}{\delta x}\right)^2}{pq^\frac{1}{2}}
\end{equation}

where $p=\left(\frac{\delta z}{\delta x}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\delta z}{\delta y}\right)^2$ and $q=1+p$ for both cases.

The input data was taken as elevation raster map (ETOPO1 raster grid covering the Kuril-Kamchatka region) using the developed methodology \cite{Mitasova1985,Hofierka2009,Horn}. The r.slope.aspect used for plotting (Fig. \ref{Fig:9}) generates raster maps of slope, aspect (computed counterclockwise from the east), profile curvature and tangential curvature from initial raster map ETOPO1 raster grid that contained elevation values. 

The input elevation raster map was specified aș ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84 the output raster map was named as ‘slope’. The computation was done by the following code: $‘r.slope.aspect elevation=ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84 \\slope=slope aspect=aspect pcurvature=pcurv tcurvature=tcurv’$. The results are shown on the facetted plot (Fig. \ref{Fig:9}) showing four subplots of geomorphic analysis of the slope, aspect, profile curvature and tangential curvature in the study area (Fig. \ref{Fig:9}).

\section{Results and Discussion}

An example of the topographic data visualization and geomorphometric analysis is provided in this study with a case of GRASS GIS based mapping of the Kuril-Kamchatka region. The study involved a ETOPO1 raster grid as a source of cartographic data including derived new information fro this initial grid: slope, aspect, curvature and general statistical data analysis (a histogram and a pie chart). Several projections have been tested and visualized using PROJ. 

The data transformation was performed using GDAL integrated with GRASS GIS. Visualisations of cartographic projections were recorded in separate plots in the GRASS GIS and later presented in a facetted plot (Fig. \ref{Fig:7}) for comparison: Cassini-Soldner equirectangular; Equal Area Cylindrical; Two Point Equidistant Azimuthal; Oblique Mercator. A range of literature sources were also examined including geology and tectonic setting of the Kuril-Kamchatka region, existing maps and published journal article on the Pacific Ocean seafloor.

Critical synthesis and analysis of a geomorphological setting of the study area is largely based on the an experiential GIS techniques of cartographic data visualization. A range of GRASS GIS modules to cartographic transformations were used to visualise better projection and colour palettes using GRASS GIS functionality. The analysis of the raster data has been done using histograms (Fig. \ref{Fig:8}). Both bar chart (left) and pie (right) histograms show frequencies of the elevation levels in a raster representing geomorphic unevenness of the study area. For each topographic range, the number of pixels in the raster image is counted and drawn into a diagram in the two views of representation. The number of pixels corresponding to the each depths/height is contributing to the height of this particular bar on the chart (or a radius of the segment in a pie). The x-axis of the bar chart diagram represents bathymetric depths (topographic heights), while the vertical y-axis shows the number of pixels counted at that particular elevation.

The results on the geomorphometric modelling show that the terrain slope in the study area (Kuril-Kamchatka region), is ranging from 0° to 39°, as shown on the calculated slope of the ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84 grid. Slope in the Kuril-Kamchatka area is shown in degrees from 0° (flat horizontal) to 39° (maximal vertical). The profile curvature shows modelled terrain surface in gradient direction which reflects the variations in the slope steepness angle with range of -87*10-5 to 68*10-5. The majority of area (coloured light green on the lower left map, Fig. \ref{Fig:9}) has values of -10*10-5 to 0. Practically, it indicates that the velocity of mass falling downwards along the slope curve. The tangential curvature reflects change in the aspect angle and influences the divergence or convergence of water flow.

The tangential curvature shows values of -67*10-5 to 63*10-5. The terrestrial areas (Kamchatka Peninsula and Greater Kuril Chain) show higher positive values (dark grey colours, lower right map in Fig. \ref{Fig:9}) comparing to the water areas of the Sea of Okhotsk (white and light grey colours) which shows variations in the land surface steepness over the terrestrial areas. Numerically, the aspect map shows the orientation in degrees from 0° to 360°  (upper right map in Fig. \ref{Fig:9}). It shows a clear correlation of the aspect with topographic area of the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench (130°-150°, emerald green colour on the map) and parallel Greater Kuril Chain (beige color, 300°-330°), both in SE orientation from the selected ‘aspectcolr’ colour palette. Clear delineation of the Sakhalin Island can be seen as NW orientation (yellow-green colours, 0°-30°) and the Kamchatka Peninsula (NW, greens, 60°-120°).

\section{Discussion}

The visualized raster maps were displayed using tested various system palettes (r.colors) specifically intended for the cartographic visualization of the terrain data. The produced maps show spatial analysis over the Kuril-Kamchatka areas performed by the GRASS GIS techniques of raster data conversion, warping and adding cartographic elements (ticks, legend, isolines) on the final layout. Each map sources a range of new visualization of the data relevant to the geomorphology of the Kuril-Kamchatka region. Similar to the GMT techniques (Lemenkova, 2020d), demonstrated method of the GRASS GIS has a command-line possibility of data input which is convenient for controlling the cartographic design and enables to create the print-quality maps. It demonstrated the effectiveness of the GRASS GIS methods to visualize raster grids and perform raster data analysis for mapping geologically complex region of the north Pacific Ocean.

The morphometry is an approach of quantitative GIS based terrain analysis, which is increasingly required by geomorphologists for relief visualization based on elevation DEM, landforms geomorphological modelling and mapping as well as in regional studies. The geomorphometric terrain analysis may include various approaches, for instance: analysis and interpretation of the land or seafloor surface as continuous field, selecting geomorphological landforms as separate study objects, extracted from a continuous field of DEM by topographic spatial analysis, slope analysis, as shown in various papers \cite{HofierkaSuri,Mitas1999,Mitasovaetal1996}. The slope gradient indicates the terrain roughness and represents basic land surface discreteness. A slope gradient can be used for indication of the terrain roughness, while the aspect can be applied for mapping slope distribution depending on the compass orientation of the terrain. 

The findings revealed that topography of the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench was varying reflecting its regional geologic evolution and tectonic setting that largely affect the structure of the relief. The isoline representation in Fig. \ref{Fig:8} was compared with previous existing research on Kuril-Kamchatka Trench \cite{Lemenkova2019a}, where the research has been performed using the combination of the GMT, Octave/MATLAB and statistical visualization of the data distribution. These research presented other source for displaying height differences using the statistical data visualization. Another example of bathymetric data processing specifically for the north Pacific Ocean area was performed using scripting approach by GMT syntax which has its specific language operated from the command line. Scripts of GMT were used to rapidly cross-section gthe bathymetric data as a series of profiles of the deep-sea trench \cite{Lemenkova2021e}. Also, the results performed a multidimensional and dynamic geographic phenomenon that can be studied using various types of data processing in GRASS GIS: visualization of the slope, aspect, curvature and statistical data distribution of elevation data.

\section{Conclusions}

The geomorphometric parameters include slope, aspect, and curvature in both the down‐slope and across‐slope directions, which is presented in this case study based on the GRASS GIS modules. The theoretical approaches in geomorphometric studies \cite{Chapman} have been implemented in practical applications of GRASS GIS and developed in GRASS module r.slope.aspect, as demonstrated in this study. The results demonstrate how the GRASS GIS can be utilised for cartographic data processing via the console by scripting to identify topographic variability in the study area through data processing, and visualise raster grids in various projections. 

The GRASS GIS modules were used for assessing the morphometrics in the land-surface parameters over the study area based on the ETOPO1-derived basin geomorphometry: profile and tangential curvature, slope and aspect maps, histograms of elevation distribution. Other examples of automatization in the cartographic processes include the machine learning approaches of rapid vectorization of the isolines \cite{Lemenkova2020e}, morphometric landscape analysis \cite{Clarke,land12010261,Sofia}, geospatial analysis based on shell scripting using scripting libraries \cite{su142315966}, topographic mapping using the GMT and QGIS \cite{Lemenkova2022a,LemenkovaDebeir2022JAES,Lemenkova2020c}, discourse on fractals in the ocean floor topography quantifying repetitive patters by modelling \cite{Malinverno}.

Unlike the traditional GIS software, the architecture of the GRASS GIS presents a collection of modules that can be stored as a sequence in a shell script and used for producing maps. In this research the GRASS GIS demonstrated effectiveness for mapping and visualization, compatibility with libraries, such as GDAL, PROJ, R \cite{Bivand2000,Bivand1999,app122412554,Grohmann}, technical flexibility combining GUI and command-line based method of data processing. Similar to GMT with existing examples of studies \cite{Lemenkova2021b}, GRASS GIS uses a scripting programming approach which enables to speed up the repeated tasks through the executable scripts. Like scripts in a UNIX shell, R and Python environments \cite{jimaging8120317}, GRASS GIS and GMT scripts \cite{Lemenkova2021g} used to automate the workflow repeatedly performed for different maps using the same algorithm for various study areas \cite{Lemenkova2022b}.
