% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{Libraries with prewritten codes optimize the workflow in cartography and reduce labour intensive data processing by iteratively applying scripts to implementing mapping tasks. Most existing GIS approaches are based on traditional software with a graphical user's interface which significantly limits their performance. Although plugins are proposed to improve the functionality of many GIS, they are usually ad hoc in finding specific mapping solutions, e.g., cartographic projections and data conversion. We address this limitation by applying the principled approach of GDAL, PROJ and GRASS GIS for geospatial data processing and morphometric analysis. This research presents topographic analysis of the dataset using scripting methods which include several tools: 1) GDAL, a translator library for raster and vector geospatial data formats used for converting ETOPO1 GeoTIFF in XY Cartesian coordinates into WGS84 by the ‘gdalwarp’ utility; 2) PROJ projection transformation library used for converting ETOPO1 WGS84 grid to cartographic projections (Cassini-Soldner equirectangular; Equal Area Cylindrical; 2-point Equidistant Azimuthal; Oblique Mercator); 3) GRASS GIS by sequential use of the following modules: r.info, d.mon, d.rast, r.colors, d.rast.leg, d.legend, d.northarrow, d.grid, d.text, g.region, r.contour. The depth frequency was analysed by the module 'd.histogram'. The proposed approach provided a systematic way for morphometric measuring of topographic data and combining the advantages of the GDAL, PROJ and GRASS GIS tools that include the informativeness, effectiveness and representativeness in spatial data processing. The morphometric analysis included the computed slope, aspect, profile and tangential curvature of the study area. The data analysis revealed the distribution pattern in topographic data: 24\% of data with elevations below 400 m; 13\% of data with depths -5,000 to -6,000 m; 4\% of depths have values -3,000 to -4,000 m, the least frequent data (-6,000 to 7,000 m) <1\%, 2\% of depths have values -2,000 to 3,000 m in the basin, while other values are distributed proportionally. Further, by incorporating the generic coordinate transformation software library PROJ, we transformed raster grid into various cartographic projections to demonstrate distortions in shape and area. Scripting techniques of GRASS GIS are demonstrated for applications in topographic modelling and raster data processing. The GRASS GIS shows the effectiveness for mapping and visualization, compatibility with libraries (GDAL, PROJ), technical flexibility in combining GUI and command-line data processing. The research contributes to the technical cartographic development.}

\section{Introduction}

The spatial analysis in GIS focuses on complex problems regarding the topographic analysis and surface modelling. Conducting data searches and capture, then finding optical GIS software with full functionality of geoinformatics, performing data visualization, analysis and modelling, processing and mapping are the vital parts of cartographic workflow, which is constantly developing in multiple application of GIS  \cite{Boulton,Buterez,Lemenkova2021a,Duncan,Lemenkova2020a,Hernandez,Kiani,Teixeira,Savulescu}. Due to the variety of the existing GIS (e.g. ArcGIS, QGIS, MapINFO, ENVI, Idrisi, SAGA GIS, Erdas Imagine or scripting approaches of GMT), the choice of the tools may face many questions regarding the advantages of the application of GIS. However, a certain advantages of GIS may be the most important factors, such as open source availability and functionality of GIS including a variety of tools and embedded computational algorithms \cite{Armstrong}. The commercial GIS (e.g. Erdas Imagine, ArcGIS) may cause frequently encountered problems of access, for example in public education or for personal usage. 

On the contrary, the free availability of GIS may cause its popularity and create an increased demand of the reported examples of its applications used in a geospatial studies \cite{Christl,Turton} including programming coding and scripting, machine learning and automatization, artificial intelligence and big data processing as pointed out in a variety of contemporary studies on geoinformatics \cite{Barnes,Issaka}. The benefits of the open source GIS and scripting are widely discussed including easily data integration in a number of scenarios and geospatial research, handling 2D and 3D raster datasets, spatial information exchange and processing \cite{Ajvazi,Brovelli,Guan,Kralidis}. 

Open source data (or at least, low cost data), scripting approaches and methods used in geographic research and Earth science education \cite{Rey,Basith,Lemenkov,Habib} tend to become progressively popular and perspective in view of the increased forms of the distance-based GIS education and flexible learning context \cite{Balram}. Common sources of open geodata are topographic and bathymetric grids (GEBCO, SRTM, ETOPO1, ETOPO5), geophysical datasets (e.g. EGM96, EGM2008), geological surveys, local data from the online repositories, embedded datasets, OpenStreetMap of Google Earth imagery, remote sensing data (e.g. Landsat TM or Sentinel-2A), professional and governmental repositories, personal findings and data received from the fieldwork.

Cartographers have access to an ever-growing pool of such datasets that focuses on discipline-specific topics (e.g. geomorphology, soils, hydrology, climate changes, land cover studies, geology, etc.) as well as various GIS software and references from other related geoscience disciplines. The open source geospatial data available online (e.g.,GEBCO, SRTM, ETOPO1, various DEM) to assist in GIS mapping are wide and varied. Many of these datasets are now available in digital formats (both raster of vector ones) and commonly accessed through the internet via geospatial data repositories. The open source geospatial data describe a collaboration of datasets in Earth sciences (topography, geology, soils, climate and environmental surveys). Increasingly, geospatial data sources are developing online repositories for their data storage such as the NOAA, GloVis (remote sensing data as satellite imagery), GEBCO, etc. Availability of geospatial data access to a cartographer is determined by their open source access (e.g. OpenStreetMaps, Google Earth, Landsat TM) or embedded datasets (country boundaries as basic GIS layers). There is a wide variety of Earth scientists who do regularly access such resources and use them in GIS-based analysis.

In view of this, the question of how to better process these data, and which GIS to select, becomes the actual and emerging question in contemporary cartography. This study presents the use of GRASS GIS, an open source powerful GIS instrument for topographic analysis, visualization, statistical data analysis and mapping with a case study of the Kuril-Kamchatka region. Besides being a free software, GRASS GIS includes a variety of functional tools for processing both raster and vector data, using both GUI and scripting approach for mapping. Leaving apart the GUI approach as more trivial and easy approach of geospatial data processing, this paper concerns the scripting methods in GRASS GIS with a special case of topographic modelling and manipulation with data using an integration of GRASS GIS with such libraries as GDAL and PROJ the key tools for processing geospatial data formats and projections. 

\section{Case Study}

The study area of this research encompasses Kuril-Kamchatka Trench and Kuril island arc which extends in north-west Pacific Ocean, from the Hokkaido Island to the Kamchatka Peninsula, Fig. \ref{Fig:1}. This region is characterised by the complex geological settings and high seismicity. Almost all of islands from the Greater Kuril Chain are located along the submarine ridge of the Kuril island arc with depths mostly 100-300 m above them. The geomorphological structures include the submerged Vityaz ridge, which extends from the oceanward side of the Sea of Okhotsk. The Kuril-Kamchatka Trench generally has a flat seabed consisting of a chain of relatively wide and narrow sections of the trench with depths of over -7,000 m. However, it differs in the southern and northern parts. The maximum depth of the trench (-9,717 m) is detected in its southwestern segment. 

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{figure}[H]
 \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{Fig_1.jpg}\\
  \caption{Study area on the ETOPO1World Global Relief Model GeoTIFF by NOAA. Mapping: GRASS GIS. Source: author.}\label{Fig:1}
\end{figure}
\vspace{12pt}

The basement surface of the Mesozoic structures near the northern coast of the Sea of Okhotsk forms a sub-latitudinal depression with depths reaching up to 3 km. Local minor trenches (depths up to -2.000 m) extend from this depression in a sub-meridional direction, covering structural uplift in the center of the sea. The southern part of the Sea of Okhotsk has a depression with depths exceeding -5,000 m. The steep basement structures of the nearby shelf and the Kuril island arc descend towards the bottom of this depression. The geomorphology of the surface of the seafloor basement in the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench correlates with its modern relief. However, its depths at the bottom of the trench seafloor are about -1,000 to -2,000 m deeper. According to the age of the constituent rocks, the Kuril-Kamchatka island arc was formed during the Oligocene period. Some of the most ancient seamounts were formed on the Mesozoic crust reached Kuril-Kamchatka Trench (located on the slopes of the trench). 

Following further subduction of the tectonic plates under island arcs, these seamounts should be cut off from the basement surface and ‘imprinted’ in the structures of the island arcs or coastal structures of active continental margins. Such volcanic mountains are found in the structures of the submarine slopes of Kamchatka Peninsula. Geophysical settings in the study area have a zone of linear positive and negative magnetic anomalies with isometric magnetic field in the Kuril basin, which extends along the Kuril island arc and continue further within the Kamchatka Peninsula. The upper edges of the anomaly-forming bodies, associated with volcanic rocks of the Pliocene-Quaternary age, lie closer to the surface. This briefly demonstrates connectivity and correlation of the submarine and terrestrial geomorphology in the geological complexity of the region of the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench and Kuril island arc.

\section{Materials and Methods}

Methodologically, the present research was implemented in the GRASS GIS software \cite{NetelerMitasova2008}. The GRASS GIS is the open source raster and vector GIS, aș well as image and graphics production system \cite{Neteleretal2008,Neteler2000}. The GRASS GIS combines the traditional GIS menu, that is, GUI with CLI run from the console, which is convenient for the user, because it allows cartographic and analytic experimentation with layout. All maps made in GRASS GIS were stored using a UTM map coordinate system within a geographic study area of the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench region. The full scripts used in this study for topographic mapping of the Kuril-Kamchatka region by the GRASS GIS are publicly available in the GitHub repository of the authors with an open access: \href{https://github.com/paulinelemenkova/Mapping_KKT_GRASS_GIS_Scripts}{https://github.com/paulinelemenkova/Mapping\_KKT\_GRASS\_GIS\_Scripts}.

The GRASS GIS uses modular system organized by name according to their functionality class (raster, vector) with the first letter indicating a function class, separated by dots and describing the specific task performed by this module.This feature is specific for GRASS GIS, similar to GMT, a scripting cartographic toolset \cite{Wesseletal2019}, which differs them from the traditional GIS. Some GIS reply on the practice of using GUI, while GRASS GIS enables to use both GUI with a standard menu and a scripting approach, which is one of the advantages of the GRASS GIS demonstrating its methodological flexibility between the fully console-based GMT and the conventional GIS. This helps to perform cartographic work in both way depending on the cartographer's preferences, particularly if they are, for example, students or the beginner learners of GIS. This paper therefore highlights the limitations in using GMT -- a fully scripting toolset and the technical advantages of GRASS GIS which profits from the combination of both approaches.

\subsection{Data capture and organization}

The data used in this project includes the open datasets available in the online sources via internet. A georeferenced TIFF file of the ETOPO1 grid by NOAA was downloaded from the publicly available source: (\href{https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/mgg/global/}{NOAA}) and used in this research as a base map following the esiting examples of the applications of NOAA products in geosciences \cite{Lemenkova2020f} (ETOPO1 Bedrock: Grid of Earth's surface depicting the bedrock underneath the ice sheets), Fig. \ref{Fig:1}. The information on the available data for raster maps was performed by the $‘g.list -f rast’$ GRASS GIS module. The information on the data grids was checked up by command ‘r.info’ module, which returns the min/max coordinates of the study area, its resolution, number of classes, cartographic projection, scale, type of map, geometry (number of centroids, points, lines, boundaries and islands), 3D or 2D. 

\vspace{6pt}

\end{figure}

The command ‘d.mon’ displays the raster map in a selected monitor: the monitors were used for the visualization of various grids by commands ‘d.mon wx0’ and ‘d.mon wx1’.
The \ac{grass} GIS project management has a certain similarity to the ArcGIS in terms of data storage: each raster or vector map consists of several auxiliary files which include various data types, categories, header, and other specific information. The information on the projection of the current mapset was checked up by commands: ‘g.proj -p’ (general description), $‘g.proj -w’$ (here the AOI extension and mathematical approximation are as follows: standard parallels, latitude of origin, central meridian, false easting and northing). 

The trial directories were removed from the Unix utility ‘rm’ from a console by commands in two steps: 1) \emph{cd /Users/pauline/grassdata} (changing to the current folder); 2) \emph{rm -r testKKT} (here: removing testing folder for the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench with all files).
The ‘g.region -p’ command was used to retrieve information on the data extension and selected region (WESN, number of rows, columns and cells, aș well aș additional cartographic data: ellipsoid, datum, zone).

\subsection{Data processing by \ac{gdal}}

The initial operations with raster file were performed by the \ac{gdal}, a translator library for raster and vector geospatial data formats (\ac{gdal} documentation: https://gdal.org/ The input to the reference list \cite{GDAL/OGR} which was run from the console of the \ac{gmt}. In general, \ac{gdal} was used to re-project raster files to the supported projection, to perform image mosaicing and transformations (Fig. \ref{Fig:2}). In this study, three \ac{gdal} utilities were used in the following code:\\ 
$‘gdalwarp’, ‘gdal\_translate’ and ‘gdalinfo’$ command (Fig. \ref{Fig:2}). The ‘gdalwarp’ utility was used to re-projection and warping of the initial original file (ETOPO1\_Bed\_g\_geotiff.tif) from XY Cartesian coordinates to the \ac{wgs84} \cite{Defense}. 

The transformation uses the \ac{epsg} number which in this case is 4326 (corresponds to the \ac{wgs84}). The warping was done by the following command: $gdalwarp -t\_srs EPSG:4326 \\ETOPO1\_Bed\_g\_geotiff.tif ETOPO1\_WGS84.tif$. The next step contains using the ‘gdal\_translate’ utility (Fig. \ref{Fig:3}) with the argument $‘-projwin\_srs <srs\_def>’$, which specifies the projwin \ac{srs} \ac{gdal} in which it interprets the coordinates (here, given coordinates in the \ac{epsg} system). 

Using the gdal\_translate (Fig. \ref{Fig:3}), the file was subset (i.e. cut off the target square area from the whole big file with clockwise going coordinates (in degrees), W=140.0000 N=60.0000 E=170.0000 S=40.0000), but not reprojected: the re-projection was done in the previous step by the ‘grdalwarp’ utility. Now the subsetting of the target area was done by the following command: $gdal\_translate -projwin 140.0000 60.0000 \\170.0000 40.0000 ETOPO1\_WGS84.tif ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84.tif.$ 

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{figure}[h]
 \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Fig_5.jpg}\\
  \caption{Raster projected from XY to \ac{wgs84} Lat/Lon coordinates with cartographic elements. Source: author.}\label{Fig:5}
\end{figure}
\vspace{12pt}

The method presentation is shown by the projection transformation by \ac{gdal} utility ‘gralwarp’ using the \ac{proj} library. It demonstrates the transformation of the \ac{tiff} file in the \ac{wgs84} geoid datum warped to a Two Point Equidistant Azimuthal projection using PROJ arguments: $gdalwarp -t\_srs '+proj=tpeqd +lat\_1=45 +lat\_2=55 +lon\_1=100 +lon\_2=120' ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84.tif ETOPO1\_KKT\_2p.tif -overwrite$. Now the file was reprojected to the \ac{wgs84} and subset (cut off) from the initial big raster grid. A gdalinfo utility was then used to check up again the correctness of the performed operations and the output data: $gdalinfo ETOPO1\_KKT\_WGS84.tif.$

The next part of the research was continued in the \ac{grass} GIS version 7.6, as described below. It included the coordinates transformation (several projections were tested) that has been done by ‘proj’. The ‘proj’ is a cartographic projection filter of the \ac{proj} software which allows converting between the geographic and projection coordinates within one datum \ac{wgs84}. The maps projections were tested in \ac{proj} via the ‘gdalwarp’ before importing them into a \ac{grass} GIS. It was possible using ‘gdalwarp’ utility of \ac{gdal}, which is specially designed to re-project maps to the target projections, coordinate systems, ellipsoid, or geodetic datum (in this case, the datum was selected as a constant \ac{wgs84} and was not changed throughout the research).
